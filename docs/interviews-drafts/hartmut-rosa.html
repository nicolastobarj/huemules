Introduction

Today we are with Hartmut Rosa, one of the most prominent social thinkers in the world. He has written many books and articles that have had a great influence on academia, politics, education and beyond. He is a professor at the Friedrich Schiller University of Jena, where we are right now: Jena, the City of Light.

How are you, Hartmut? Are you ready to be interviewed by a huemul?

I could extend this introduction with a long list of Professor Rosa’s achievements, publications and positions, but that would be far too conventional for a puppet like me. Chile is the country of the poets, so I prepared a short poem to introduce Hartmut to our audience:

Born in the rural
A man of these times
Accelerate structural
In London his mind
He noted the rhythms were others
While he was feeling another
An idea was born in the corner
By the German with his English brothers

His surname is a flower
With some thorns and petals
Surprising with the power
Of his sociology of metal

Between sharp dualisms
He rebels against ostracism
With the resonant axes
As modernity advances

Now he reaches new fields
With the energy as yield
Questions follow each other
With these insights discovered

Okay. No more introductions. First question, quickly: how do you feel about the poem?

As you may have noticed, the poem touches on different events, stages and phases of your life. I want to start by talking about you. Don’t distract us with theory, abstract concepts or anything like that. Let’s talk about modern’s favorite word: let’s talk about YOU. What happened in your life that awakened your inclination to think about society, its problems and its dilemmas? How did a German child from a small town become a famous intellectual?

Making sense of the world for people through a theory of how modern society works is a big deal. It is not easy to capture the “Zeitgeist”, the spirit of our times, in a polished concept. It takes a lot of time reading, taking notes, connecting ideas. On top of that, in academia, social thought is losing ground to growing publication requirements, demands for methodological innovation and empirical studies, and the fast pace of research in general. What problems did you face on your path to becoming a social theorist? How did you overcome them? Based on your experience, what would you say to young people who are pursuing similar dreams?

Dynamic Stabilization and new generations

Well, Let’s talk about your theories. Your work began to gain international recognition with your book “Beschleunigung” or “Social Acceleration”, published in 2005. In this book, you argue that the heart of the problems of modern societies lies in “dynamic stabilization”: the need of almost all our organizations, institutions and structures to grow, innovate and accelerate their processes just to stay as they are. Can you tell us more about this condition? How is dynamic stabilization expressed in the problems we face today, like the crisis of democracy, psychological burnout or ecological issues?

In a more recent book, “Late Modernity in Crisis”, you said we need another way of organizing our institutions, which you call “adaptive stabilization”. This concept hasn’t been explored in great depth in your work, but it seems to correspond to a “structural” solution that you propose for modernity. Could you tell us more about it? And how could adaptive stabilization work as an antidote to dynamic stabilization?

I think all good theories have to be compatible with human freedom. We social theorists have not found the “truth” or definitive laws of human coexistence; what we can offer is a solid and rigorous interpretation of our times. In the conclusions of “Social Acceleration”, you tell the reader that you don’t have clear answers for coping with the collapse that speed is carrying us toward. But at the same time, you don’t take a pessimistic view. After weighing all the possible alternatives, you encourage new generations of social theorists to look for creative ideas that could unpredictably change the course of events. To illustrate this, you use a phrase from Bourdieu that I find beautiful. I will quote it:

“When it is profound and consistent, sociology is not satisfied with a kind of mere observation that could be demoralizing to work against the inmanent tendencies of the social order. And whoever calls the deterministic should recall that the law of gravity must first be known by those who build the flying machines that effectively overcome it.”

My generation, Gen Z, have seen how climate change and the development of AI are pushing to the limit the contradictions you identified in your early books. But there are so many challenges that sometimes we feel lost on how to change the status quo. It is the duty of sociologists to guide these questions for the general public. In that sense, what actions or missions do you think the younger generation, who are now inheriting a world where acceleration has reached its limit, should take on? What can you see in the new generations which will have to make the transition to adaptive stabilization?

Many political analysts pointed out that Generation Z was taking a turn toward conservatism, but the truth is that right now they are leading a series of uprisings around the world: We saw it in Chile in 2019, in Nepal last year, and in France today. It seems we are in an era of great pent-up energy that cannot be gradually channeled. All the discontent erupts, and that prevents this energy from being channeled into concrete institutional changes—only into frustrated protests. Everything changes rapidly, so that in the end nothing changes. I find it interesting that you’ve decided to start systematically studying the concept of “social energy” at this time. Why do you think this concept is fundamental to understand our era? In what consists? and how could help new generations to reach the changes they want?

But social energy is not only burning out in this type of left mobilizations. Now we are watching how in the world there’s far-right movements that start to take the power by electoral-competitive methods. The discomfort is moving to neo-fascism, and now are a realistic alternative for many Gen Z people. Can you distinct aggresive forms of social energy from others that foster transcendence and inclusion?

Resonances, Social Energy and chilean culture

Now let’s talk about alienation and resonance. A Chilean article by Professor Montero and Professor Torres reconstructs your theoretical journey, looking back at how, starting from your early work on acceleration, you arrived at the idea of an alternative of alienation based on resonance. You have always defined four very clear dimensions of resonance and how they relate to each other. Could you tell us why resonance could be an alternative to the alienation experience generated by dynamic stabilization? How does it work?

In a recently published interview with Siyu Yao, you talked about how important it is to have a “secure home base” from which to set out into the world and be able to be touched and transformed by things or people you don’t know. In my case, I come from Chile, an isolated small land surrounded by mountains, deserts and the Pacific Ocean. For a Patagonian Chilean huemul like me, it has been emotionally difficult to come here and understand how a new culture works, without the support of the family and friends I’ve had my whole life. I think achieving resonant relationships requires going through painful situations again and again. Resonance demands to transform yourself, to leave behind your comfortable previous way of being in the world. By this pain I don’t mean alienation, because it is not an empty or silent void. It is the inherent chaos of a process of change: the deregulation and uncertainty in your inner life that comes with opening up to new experiences. So, how do you deal with the pain of losing your previous self in your theory of resonance?

In a way, by promoting resonant relationships, we, social scientists, advocate for a certain degree of entropy in the social order. This is a very German warning. Hölderlin tells us that where the danger is, there also grows what saves us. It strikes me that these ideas came from a society known for its abundance of bureaucratic rules and regulations. Do you think this point of view is related to the German culture you come from? What do you think makes the difference between a positive transformation of oneself, when one’s axes of resonance are broken, and the silencing of the world?

Well, I know you have been in Santiago and Valparaíso for conferences, and that you have also supervised some doctoral students from Chile. Our land is known for the constant danger of natural disasters. We live with frequent earthquakes, tsunamis, floods and so on. Gabriela Mistral, our most prominent poet, said that the most representative element of the Chilean “soul” is the will to be. By this she meant something very similar to Nietzsche’s idea of will: Chile is a land without comfortable conditions for existence, but even so, stubborn people decided to make this threatening but beautiful territory their own. Chile wants to be, despite the lack of resources and the foretold, inevitable moment of collapse. As a result, Chilean culture tends to value simple things. We know that big, monumental things can fall apart tomorrow, so we prefer to look at what lies beneath. This is why many of our philosophers, such as Giannini, Maturana or Jorge Millas, focus on questions of everyday life instead of metaphysical issues. We are known for writers as Nicanor Parra, the “anti-poet”, who brought verse down from Olympus with direct, simple and ironic language. Our way of channeling social energy tends to be about doing a lot with a little. Did you feel the same during your visits to Chile? Comparing other parts of the world, how do you think social energy and resonance are experienced in Chile?

When I think about resonance, I remember a line by a very young Chilean indie band called “Inundaremos”. In one of their most emotional songs, the lyrics say: “Tenderness is stepping outside of yourself to face the world, allowing it to caress you, while also exposing yourself to the risk that it might break you.” I think your concept of resonance contains a call to be more tender. Well, our national coat of arms has two animals: the condor, a bird similar to the eagle, and the huemul, a small deer like me. In 1925, Gabriela Mistral wrote the text “Less Condor, More Huemul” to tell our people that, to find our own culture, we should focus more on the tenderness of the huemul than on the strength and military domination of the condor. Do you think tenderness has a role in resonance? And do you feel it is necessary for the future of humanity?

The German coat of arms shows an eagle on a golden background. Sometimes it has been used to symbolize the value of reason and democracy; at other times, for expansionist purposes. Thinking about your theories, how would you interpret this symbol for your nation? What message do you feel it carries?

I believe that German eagles and huemules are brothers. There are many examples of Chileans who have been inspired by Germans in their creative endeavors. In 1889, Rudolf Lenz, a German philologist influenced by Herder’s vision of the “Volksgeist”, arrived in Chile. He was one of the first intellectuals to tell our people that we should value our own way of speaking and expressing ourselves, instead of imitating the European aristocratic culture. Lenz’s message inspired many artist to appreciate our own simplicity, as the folk musician Violeta Parra. Do you think social energy can be transmitted between different cultures? How could this help us solve the problems of humanity?

Now I want to show you a short video. It is about a cumbia band called “Chico Trujillo”. Looking for creative inspiration and a receptive audience, they decided to live in Berlin during the 2000s. There they created a new and completely unique style of cumbia that Chile now enjoys a lot. “Macha”, the leader of the band, is a well-known punk figure in Chile, and in Germany too, as he played many times at Kunsthaus Tacheles and at SO36 in Kreuzberg. In this video, they walk around Berlin enjoying the moment. Watch it and tell me how this cooperation between Chile and Germany represents possible transmisions of energy between cultures.
