# Introduction

Today we are with Hartmut Rosa, one of the most prominent social thinkers in the world. He has written many books and articles that have had a great influence on academia, politics, education and beyond. He is a professor at the Friedrich Schiller University of Jena, where we are right now: Jena, the City of Light.

How are you, Hartmut? Are you ready to be interviewed by a huemul?

---

I could extend this introduction with a long list of Professor Rosa's achievements, publications, and positions, but that would be far too conventional for a puppet like me. So I have prepared a short poem to introduce Hartmut to our audience:

Born in the countryside
A man of these times
Accelerating structures
With his mind in London
He noticed rhythms unlike his own
As he sensed something different
An idea was born in a corner
By a German among his English brothers

His surname is a flower
With thorns and petals
Surprising in its power
Of his sociology of metal

Between sharp dualisms
He rebels against ostracism
With his axes of resonance
As modernity advances

Though the world is uncontrollable
He is not left uncomfortable
Now he reaches new fields
With energy as his yield

When I look through his books
Theory offers me some clues
Questions follow each other
As new insights are discovered

Okay. No more introductions. First question, quickly: how do you feel about the poem?

---

As you may have noticed, the poem touches on different events, stages, and phases of your life. I want to start by talking about you. Don't distract us with theory, abstract concepts, or anything like that. Let's talk about modernity's favorite word: let's talk about YOU. What happened in your life that awakened your inclination to think about society, its problems, and its dilemmas? How did a German child from a small town become Professor Hartmut Rosa?

---

Making sense of the world for others through a theory of how modern society works is a big undertaking. It is not easy to capture the "Zeitgeist," the spirit of our times, in a polished concept. It takes a great deal of reading, note-taking, and connecting ideas. On top of that, in academia, social thought is losing ground amid growing publication requirements, demands for methodological innovation and empirical research, and the increasingly fast pace of research. What problems did you face on your path to becoming a social theorist? How did you overcome them? Based on your experience, what would you say to young people pursuing similar dreams?

---

# Dynamic Stabilization and new generations

Now comes the moment everyone has been waiting for. Let's talk about your theories. Your work began to gain international recognition with your book *Beschleunigung*, published in 2005 and translated into English in 2013 as *Social Acceleration*. In this book, you argue that the heart of modern societies' problems lies in "dynamic stabilization": the need for almost all our organizations, institutions, and structures to grow, innovate, and accelerate their processes just to stay as they are. Can you tell us more about this condition? How is dynamic stabilization expressed in the problems we face today, such as the crisis of democracy, psychological burnout, and ecological issues?

---

In a more recent book, *Late Modernity in Crisis*, you said we need another way of organizing our institutions, which you call "adaptive stabilization." This concept has not been explored in great depth in your work, but it seems to correspond to a "structural," or third-person, solution that you propose for modernity. Could you tell us more about it? What do you mean by a third-person approach? And how could adaptive stabilization work as an antidote to dynamic stabilization?

---

I think all good theories have to be compatible with human freedom. We social theorists have not found the "truth" or definitive laws of human coexistence; what we can offer is a solid and rigorous interpretation of our times. In the conclusion of *Social Acceleration*, you tell readers that you do not have clear answers for coping with the collapse that speed is carrying us toward. At the same time, however, you do not take a pessimistic view. After weighing the possible alternatives, you encourage new generations of social theorists to look for creative ideas that could unpredictably change the course of events. To illustrate this, you use a phrase from Bourdieu that I find beautiful. I will quote it:

"When it is profound and consistent, sociology is not satisfied with a kind of mere observation that could be demoralizing to work against the immanent tendencies of the social order. And whoever calls the deterministic should recall that the law of gravity must first be known by those who build the flying machines that effectively overcome it."

My generation, Gen Z, has seen how climate change and the development of AI are pushing the contradictions you identified in your early books to their limits. There are so many challenges, however, that we sometimes feel lost about how to change the status quo. Sociologists have a duty to bring these questions to the general public. In that sense, what actions or missions do you think younger generations, who are now inheriting a world where acceleration has reached its limit, should take on? What do you see in the new generations who will have to make the transition to adaptive stabilization?

---

Many political analysts have pointed out that Generation Z is turning toward conservatism. Yet right now, young people are leading a series of uprisings around the world. We saw it in Chile in 2019, in Nepal last year, and in France today. It seems we are in an era of pent-up energy that cannot be channeled gradually. Discontent erupts, preventing this energy from being directed toward concrete institutional change and leaving it to spill into frustrated protests. I find it interesting that you have decided to begin systematically studying the concept of "social energy" at this time. Why do you think this concept is fundamental to understanding our era? What does it consist of, and how could it help new generations achieve the changes they want?

---

# Resonance, Social Energy, and Chilean Culture

Now let's talk about alienation and resonance. An article by Chilean scholars Professor Montero and Professor Torres reconstructs your theoretical journey, tracing how you moved from your early work on acceleration to the idea of resonance as an alternative to alienation. Some other German thinkers, such as Axel Honneth and Rahel Jaeggi, have criticized this concept as ambiguous. Yet you have clearly defined four dimensions of resonance and explained how they relate to one another. Could you tell us why resonance could be an alternative to the experience of alienation generated by dynamic stabilization? How does it work?

---

In a recently published interview with Siyu Yao, you talked about how important it is to have a "secure home base" from which to set out into the world and be touched and transformed by unfamiliar things and people. In my case, I come from Chile, a small, isolated country surrounded by mountains, deserts, and the Pacific Ocean. For a Patagonian Chilean huemul like me, it has been emotionally difficult to come here and understand how a new culture works without the support of the family and friends who have been by my side my whole life. I think building resonant relationships requires going through painful situations again and again. Resonance requires transforming yourself and leaving behind your previous, more comfortable way of being in the world. By this pain, I do not mean alienation, because it is not an empty or silent void. It is the inherent chaos of a process of change: the disruption and uncertainty in your inner life that come with opening up to new experiences. In a way, by promoting resonant relationships, we social scientists advocate for a certain degree of entropy in the social order. This is a very German warning. Hölderlin tells us that where the danger is, there also grows what saves us. It strikes me that these ideas came from a society known for its abundance of bureaucratic rules and regulations. So, how do you deal with the pain of losing your previous self in your theory of resonance? Do you think this point of view is related to the German culture you come from? What do you think makes the difference between a positive self-transformation, when one's axes of resonance are disrupted, and the silencing of the world?

---

I know you have been to Santiago and Valparaíso for conferences, and that you have also supervised some doctoral students from Chile. Our land is known for the constant threat of natural disasters. We live with frequent earthquakes, tsunamis, floods, and more. Gabriela Mistral, our most prominent poet, said that the most representative element of the Chilean "soul" is the will to be. By this she meant something very similar to Nietzsche's idea of will: Chile is a land without comfortable conditions for existence, yet stubborn people have chosen to make this threatening but beautiful territory their own. Chile wants to be, despite its lack of resources and the foretold, inevitable moment of collapse. As a result, Chilean culture tends to value simple things. We know that big, monumental things can fall apart tomorrow, so we prefer to look at what lies beneath. This is why many of our philosophers, such as Giannini, Maturana, and Jorge Millas, focus on questions of everyday life instead of metaphysical issues. We are also known for writers such as Nicanor Parra, the "anti-poet," who brought verse down from Olympus with direct, simple, and ironic language. Our way of channeling social energy tends to involve doing a lot with a little. Did you feel the same during your visits to Chile? Compared with other parts of the world, how do you think social energy and resonance are experienced in Chile?

---

When I think about resonance, I remember a line by a very young Chilean indie band called "Inundaremos." In one of their most emotional songs, the lyrics say: "Tenderness is stepping outside of yourself to face the world, allowing it to caress you, while also exposing yourself to the risk that it might break you." I think your concept of resonance contains a call to be more tender. Our national coat of arms has two animals: the condor, a bird similar to the eagle, and the huemul, a small deer like me. In 1925, Gabriela Mistral wrote the essay "Less Condor, More Huemul" to tell our people that, to find our own culture, we should focus more on the tenderness of the huemul than on the strength and military domination of the condor. Do you think tenderness has a role in resonance? And do you feel it is necessary for the future of humanity?

---

The German coat of arms shows an eagle on a golden background. At times, it has symbolized the values of reason and democracy; at others, it has been used for expansionist purposes. Today you are a cultural figure for the German people. Thinking about your theories, how would you interpret this symbol for your nation? What message do you feel it carries?

---

I believe that German eagles and huemules are brothers. There are many examples of Chileans who have been inspired by Germans in their creative endeavors. In 1889, Rudolf Lenz, a German philologist influenced by Herder's vision of the "Volksgeist," arrived in Chile. He was one of the first intellectuals to tell our people that we should value our own way of speaking and expressing ourselves instead of imitating European aristocratic culture. Lenz's message inspired many artists to appreciate our own simplicity, including the folk musician Violeta Parra. Do you think social energy can be transmitted between different cultures? How could this help us solve humanity's problems?

---

Now I want to show you a short video. It is about a cumbia band called "Chico Trujillo." Looking for creative inspiration and a receptive audience, they decided to live in Berlin during the 2000s. There, they created a new and completely unique style of cumbia that Chile now enjoys. "Macha," the band's leader, is a well-known punk figure in Chile and Germany, where he played many times at Kunsthaus Tacheles and SO36 in Kreuzberg. In this video, they walk around Berlin and enjoy the moment. Watch it and tell me how this cooperation between Chile and Germany represents the possible transmission of energy between cultures.

I think the song's lyrics may have been inspired by your work, haha, because Macha sings that what doesn't need to be rushed takes longer to achieve. I think that is true: we have to be more patient in pursuing our desires.

Is there a last message you would like to share with Chilean and Latin American youth?
