# Dynamic Stabilization and new generations

Now comes the moment everyone has been waiting for. Let's talk about your theories. Your work began to gain international recognition with your book "Beschleunigung", published in 2005 and translated into English in 2013 as "Social Acceleration". In this long essay, you argue that the heart of the problems of modern societies lies in "dynamic stabilization": the need of almost all our organizations, institutions and structures to grow, innovate and accelerate their processes just to stay as they are. Can you tell us more about this condition? How is dynamic stabilization expressed in the problems we face today, like the crisis of democracy, psychological burnout or ecological issues?

---

In a more recent book, "Late Modernity in Crisis", you said we need another way of organizing our institutions, which you call "adaptive stabilization". This concept hasn't been explored in great depth in your work, but it seems to correspond to a "structural", or third-person, solution that you propose for modernity. Could you tell us more about it? What do you mean by a third-person approach? And how could adaptive stabilization work as an antidote to dynamic stabilization?

---

I think all good theories have to be compatible with human freedom. We social theorists have not found the "truth" or definitive laws of human coexistence; what we can offer is a solid and rigorous interpretation of our times. In the conclusions of "Social Acceleration", you tell the reader that you don't have clear answers for coping with the collapse that speed is carrying us toward. But at the same time, you don't take a pessimistic view. After weighing all the possible alternatives, you encourage new generations of social theorists to look for answers that could unpredictably change the course of events. One thing young people have in common is that they know the world is in bad shape, but they're at a loss as to how to change it. It is the duty of sociologists to help guide these questions for the general public. # Cultural exchange Chile-Germany

In a recently published interview with Siyu Yao, you talked about how important it is to have a "secure home base" from which to set out into the world and be able to be touched and transformed by things or people you don't know. In my case, I come from Chile, an isolated land surrounded by mountains, deserts and the Pacific Ocean. Because of this historical isolation, we have very particular customs and expressions. For a Patagonian Chilean huemul like me, it has been emotionally difficult to come here and understand how a new culture works, without the support of the family and friends I've had my whole life. Many people from small and peripheral countries travel to the developed centers of the world looking for opportunities, but the loneliness of being far from home can easily turn their experience in unknown lands into an alienated one. What do you think makes the difference between a positive transformation of oneself, when one's axes of resonance are broken, and the silencing of the world? What advice would you give to people who pursue their dreams far from home?

---

I think achieving resonant relationships requires going through painful situations again and again. Resonance demands that you transform yourself, that you leave behind your previous way of being in the world. In a sense, this process means losing part of your identity, of what you understand as normal and correct, and also separating from the places and people you were used to. By this pain I don't mean alienation, because it is not an empty or silent void. It is the inherent chaos of a process of change: the deregulation and uncertainty in your inner life that comes with opening up to new experiences. In a way, by promoting resonant relationships, we social scientists advocate for a certain degree of entropy in the social order. This is a very German warning. Hölderlin tells us that where the danger is, there also grows what saves us. It strikes me that these ideas came from a society known for its abundance of bureaucratic rules and regulations. So, how do you deal with the pain of losing your previous self in your theory of resonance? Do you think this point of view is related to the German culture you come from?

---

I know you have been to Santiago and Valparaíso for conferences, and that you have also supervised some doctoral students from Chile. From all these experiences, tell me: what did you discover about Chile? Is there something particular about our culture that caught your attention?

---

To the isolation I mentioned before, we could add the constant danger of natural disasters. Our land is under great tectonic stress because of the collision between the Nazca and South American plates. We live with frequent earthquakes, tsunamis, floods and so on. Gabriela Mistral, our most prominent poet, said that the most representative element of the Chilean "soul" is the will to be. By this she meant something very similar to Nietzsche's idea of will: Chile is a land without comfortable conditions for existence, but even so, stubborn people decided to make this threatening but beautiful territory their own. Chile wants to be, despite the lack of resources and the foretold, inevitable moment of collapse. As a result, Chilean culture tends to value simple things. We know that big, monumental things can fall apart tomorrow, so we prefer to look at what lies beneath. This is why many of our philosophers, such as Giannini, Maturana or Jorge Millas, focus on questions of everyday life instead of metaphysical issues. We are known for poets like Pablo Neruda, who wrote about simple things like the sound of seagulls or a typical Chilean dish like "caldillo de congrio", and Nicanor Parra, the "anti-poet", who brought verse down from Olympus with direct, simple and ironic language. Our way of channeling social energy tends to be about doing a lot with a little. Did you feel the same during your visits to Chile? Do you think social energy and resonance are experienced differently in Chile, in Europe, or in the Asian countries you have visited? Could you relate the uncontrollability of the world to this kind of human expression we have?

---

When I think about resonance, I remember a line by a very young Chilean indie band called "Inundaremos". In one of their most emotional songs, the lyrics say: "Tenderness is stepping outside of yourself to face the world, allowing it to caress you, while also exposing yourself to the risk that it might break you." I think your concept of resonance contains a call to be more tender. Well, our national coat of arms has two animals: the condor, a bird similar to the eagle, and the huemul, a small deer like me. In 1925, Gabriela Mistral wrote the text "Less Condor, More Huemul" to tell our people that, to find our own culture, we should focus more on the tenderness of the huemul than on the strength and military domination of the condor. Do you think tenderness has a role in resonance? And do you feel it is necessary for the future of humanity?

---

The German coat of arms shows an eagle on a golden background. Sometimes it has been used to symbolize the value of reason and democracy; at other times, for expansionist purposes. Today you are a cultural figure for German people. Thinking about your theories, how would you interpret this symbol for your nation? What message do you feel it carries?

---

I believe that German eagles and huemules are brothers. There are many examples of Chileans who have been inspired by Germans in their creative endeavors. In 1889, Rudolf Lenz, a German philologist influenced by Herder's vision of the "Volksgeist", arrived in Chile. He was one of the first intellectuals to tell our people that we should value our own way of speaking and expressing ourselves, instead of copying the elite, who were always trying to imitate European culture. Lenz's message inspired the folk music of Violeta Parra and many writers in the first half of the twentieth century. This small gesture was one of the first things that helped us begin to appreciate our own simplicity. Do you think social energy can be transmitted between different cultures? How could this help us solve the problems of humanity?
